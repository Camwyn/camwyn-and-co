The stories that last are rarely the ones about grand triumphs.

They are the small, specific recollections: the kitchen recipe written on the back of an envelope in pencil, the worn wooden mallet that has outlived three workshops, the late-night drive home through summer rain with the windows cracked open.

## The Archival Instinct

Every family, studio, and company generates an immense volume of life every day. Most of it slips quietly away simply because no one took a moment to write it down, photograph the process, or ask the person sitting across from them how they learned to do what they do.

> "To remember what mattered is to give the next generation a foundation rather than an empty room."

When you document the origins of things—why a venture was named, where the ingredients came from, whose hands shaped the wood—you endow the work with a soul that cannot be manufactured.

## Keeping the Chronicle Alive

- **Ask the second question:** When someone mentions a past experience, don't stop at the summary. Ask: *"What did that look like? How did that feel?"*
- **Keep the physical records:** Keep paper letters, hand-drawn diagrams, and printed menus. Digital archives are convenient, but tangible artifacts carry weight.
- **Pass it along:** A story kept to yourself is just a memory. A story shared at the table becomes tradition.

Every tale is worth remembering—and retelling.
